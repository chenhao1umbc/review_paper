## Section 5: Technical and Translational Challenges

The TRL 3–4 state documented in Section 4 reflects a specific constellation of barriers that are neither uniform nor independently solvable. Some are technical: manipulation precision, contact safety during bipedal locomotion, software validation against clinical performance standards. Some are institutional: regulatory pathway ambiguity, liability framework gaps, actuarial absence. And some are structurally circular: regulatory approval requires clinical evidence; clinical evidence requires IRB-sanctioned trials; IRB approval requires safety assurance; safety assurance requires performance standards that regulatory guidance has not yet established. This section diagnoses each barrier specifically and identifies what progress would look like.

---

### 5.1 Manipulation and Dexterity for Clinical Tools

\cite{atar2025humanoids} is the only study to systematically document manipulation performance gaps in a clinical task context. Atar et al. evaluated a Unitree G1 with Inspire Gen4 dexterous hands across seven clinical procedures, achieving approximately 70% aggregate procedural success with non-clinician teleoperators. That aggregate conceals a sharp performance gradient: auscultation and ultrasound — tasks requiring sustained positional contact only — achieved high success rates, while IV catheter insertion and blood glucose finger-stick testing were markedly less reliable. The paper identifies two specific failure modes. First, force output insufficiency: the actuator configuration cannot reliably generate the controlled forces required for needle penetration (IV insertion requires a precisely metered advance through skin and vessel wall) or sustained grip against resistance (bag-valve mask compression requires repeatable forces over multiple ventilation cycles). Second, sensor sensitivity limitations: the tactile feedback loop is insufficient for sub-Newton force resolution, preventing the robot from detecting the haptic signatures — resistance drop at vessel entry, tissue compliance transitions — that guide needle procedures in clinical practice.

These findings generalize across the procedure literature. \cite{liang2025lapsurgie} demonstrates that laparoscopic surgery requires enforcing a remote-center-of-motion (RCM) constraint at the trocar site: an instrument entering the abdomen must pivot around a fixed point in space, and any deviation applies lateral force to the port wound. The inverse-mapping strategy in LapSurgie enforces RCM as a kinematic constraint in software — an engineering workaround for a capability that commercial humanoid arms do not provide natively. \cite{cho2026humanoid} identifies three engineering targets for endoscopic clinical translation that remain unmet: endoscope stability in confined anatomical cavities, force compliance when the instrument contacts tissue, and positioning accuracy sufficient for the surgeon to rely on the camera view.

Synthesizing across P1, P2, and P3, clinical manipulation requires three capabilities that current commercial humanoid arms do not yet reliably provide: (1) force precision at sub-Newton resolution for needle and catheter procedures; (2) tool-specific kinematic adaptation — laparoscopes, rigid endoscopes, and ultrasound probes have geometric requirements (RCM constraints, fixed shaft lengths, specific handle orientations) that general-purpose end-effectors do not address by default; and (3) fingertip-level tactile sensing for grip force discrimination, instrument slip detection, and tissue compliance sensing. Operationally, progress requires force resolution at sub-Newton levels for needle insertion procedures \cite{atar2025humanoids}, fingertip sensor density exceeding 1,000 sensing elements, and teleoperation round-trip latency at levels consistent with real-time force feedback requirements — thresholds established for surgical teleoperation systems and typically cited in the range of 200 ms or below.

### 5.2 Safe Physical Contact and Bipedal Gait Dynamics

Progress in contact safety has been demonstrated. \cite{lu2025gentlehumanoid} introduces a spring-based compliance framework for the Unitree G1 that quantifiably reduces peak contact forces during quasi-static tasks including sit-to-stand assistance and upper-body physical contact. This is the most technically grounded progress toward patient-contact safety in the current literature.

The design target for patient-contact force limits is the Power and Force Limiting (PFL) regime of ISO/TS 15066:2016, which provides biomechanically derived thresholds for different body regions: for the face and skull, quasi-static contact force is limited to 65 N and pressure to 110 N/cm²; for the trunk and thorax, 140 N and 210 N/cm². The GentleHumanoid framework demonstrates a Unitree G1 approaching these thresholds for quasi-static upper-body contact. That is meaningful progress, and ISO/TS 15066 PFL is the applicable safety regime for a standing humanoid interacting with a seated or supine patient.

The critical gap, however, is structural. ISO/TS 15066 was designed for fixed-base collaborative robots — cobots whose base is anchored, whose motion is constrained to a defined workspace, and whose approach velocities are programmed. It models intentional manipulation and controlled approach. It does not model contact forces arising from bipedal locomotion. A bipedal humanoid navigating a clinical environment introduces four risk scenarios that ISO/TS 15066 does not bound: (1) balance corrections, in which whole-body torque adjustments to prevent a fall generate rapid impulse forces that can contact nearby persons; (2) stumble recovery, in which momentum transfer across the whole body creates swinging limbs and torso rotation at velocities above the standard's contact speed assumptions; (3) narrow-corridor navigation, in which swinging arms during gait will contact bed rails, IV poles, and patient limbs in spaces with less than one body-width of clearance; and (4) postural transitions, in which a full-size humanoid (35 kg or more) rising from or lowering to a working height near a supine patient generates dynamics that quasi-static force limits do not characterize.

None of the 14 papers reviewed addresses this category of risk, and neither ISO/TS 15066, ISO 13482, nor ISO 10218-1/2 was designed to model or bound gait-induced contact events. This is an open safety engineering problem. Resolution requires either new standards specific to bipedal humanoid robots in patient-contact environments, or architectural solutions — proximity-triggered gait interrupts, patient-detection zones constraining locomotion near occupied beds, whole-body collision prediction for gait trajectories — that have not yet been developed in the published literature.

### 5.3 Human-Robot Trust and Institutional Adoption

Trust and adoption operate at three distinct layers that require separate analysis.

The first layer is patient and clinician acceptance. \cite{robinson2023brief} shows that 53% of approached general-population participants agreed to engage with an autonomous humanoid for a wellbeing intervention (pilot RCT, n=230). \cite{sayis2024technology} shows that the humanoid condition — compared to a voice-only interface — was the only condition producing measurable mood improvements in a therapeutic writing task (n=42). Both results are bounded: the clinical populations central to humanoid deployment — frail elderly patients with cognitive impairment, post-surgical patients, pediatric patients — differ substantially from the general and educational populations in these studies. The assumption that acceptance findings transfer to vulnerable clinical populations is untested.

The second layer is co-design and workflow integration. \cite{ghosh2024usercentered} demonstrates that iterative co-design with care staff improves system usability. This is a methodological contribution establishing the correct design process; it does not substitute for clinical evidence of safety and effectiveness. Trust in clinical technology accrues from demonstrated performance over time, not from the process by which a system was designed.

The third and most institutionally consequential layer is the liability gap. No current healthcare liability framework — US tort law, UK clinical negligence, EU product liability — assigns accountability for harm caused by an autonomous robotic action in a care setting. If a humanoid assistant causes injury during a patient transfer or procedure, liability is distributed across a chain of parties (manufacturer, institution, supervising clinician, software developer) that existing frameworks were not designed to handle. This legal ambiguity deters adoption regardless of technical capability: hospital risk departments cannot quantify institutional exposure for systems operating in legally uncharted territory. The downstream consequence is actuarial: medical liability insurers have no basis for pricing humanoid robot risk in the absence of deployment data. \cite{imtiaz2024perceptions} documents this precisely — 269 nursing home and long-term care administrators cite cost, reduction in human contact, and unproven clinical effectiveness as primary barriers. These are correct characterizations of the current state. None is a technical problem; all three require clinical evidence, legal clarification, or both.

### 5.4 Regulatory Pathway

The regulatory challenge is not hostility to this technology class; it is a jurisdictional gap that existing frameworks leave precisely where clinical humanoids sit.

ISO 13482:2014 (Safety Requirements for Personal Care Robots) is the most directly applicable safety standard — it governs robots providing physical assistance in human environments. However, § 1.4 of ISO 13482 explicitly excludes devices regulated as medical devices. A humanoid robot performing clinical tasks — IV insertion, patient transfer, rehabilitation assistance, diagnostic auscultation — almost certainly meets the medical device definition under FDA 21 CFR § 201(h) and EU MDR Article 2(1), which capture any apparatus with a declared medical intended use. This creates a hard gap: the system is likely too clinical for ISO 13482 but no ISO medical robotics standard covers general-purpose bipedal humanoid assistants. No standard currently governs a clinical humanoid assistant end-to-end.

In the United States, the De Novo classification pathway is the only viable regulatory entry route. No 510(k) predicate exists — there is no legally marketed bipedal humanoid performing clinical tasks. A De Novo submission requires the applicant to propose Special Controls from scratch: accepted performance standards for fall prevention in patient-care environments, patient contact force limits for dynamic gait scenarios, teleoperation latency bounds for specific procedure types, and AI/ML software validation methodology for clinical manipulation policies. None of these currently exist as accepted standards. The applicant must propose and defend all of them, without the support of a prior cleared device with post-market safety data. This is not procedurally impossible — De Novo has established new device categories for surgical planning software and exoskeletal rehabilitation devices — but it represents a development and regulatory investment no humanoid manufacturer has yet made.

The structural problem is a circular dependency: approval requires clinical evidence; human-subject evidence requires IRB approval and institutional support; IRB approval requires safety assurance; safety assurance requires performance standards; performance standards require regulatory guidance. The productive path through this circle is a staged evidence generation sequence — in vitro feasibility, cadaver study, IRB-approved human-subject feasibility trial — producing the data needed for a De Novo submission. \cite{cho2026humanoid} is the current frontier of that sequence: a cadaveric surgical study establishing the first structured pre-clinical evidence for humanoid surgical assistance. The next step — a registered human-subject feasibility study — has not been announced.

In the EU, a humanoid clinical assistant performing monitoring or physical assistance would likely be classified as Class IIa under EU MDR Annex VIII, with autonomous surgical assistance escalating to Class IIb or III. EU AI Act Regulation 2024/1689 additionally classifies any AI-driven clinical humanoid as a high-risk AI system, imposing risk management, data governance, logging, and human oversight obligations. High-risk AI rules apply from August 2026; AI embedded in regulated medical products transitions to August 2027. Any EU clinical humanoid market entry must satisfy both MDR and AI Act simultaneously — a dual compliance burden for which no completed template yet exists.

### 5.5 Software Infrastructure and AI Validation

The open-source foundation is capable. The leading VLA models — openpi/π₀ (10,551 stars), OpenVLA (5,479 stars), and the LeRobot framework (22,179 stars) — represent the highest-capability general-purpose robot learning infrastructure publicly available. Genesis (28,254 stars) and IsaacLab (6,544 stars) provide physics simulation for humanoid training. The Unitree ecosystem supports direct G1 hardware integration including teleoperation and imitation learning pipelines. For general-purpose humanoid manipulation research, this infrastructure is genuinely strong.

What is absent is the layer between this general infrastructure and clinical deployment. Four gaps have no current open-source solution: (1) a clinical safety layer — no ROS package enforces patient-contact safety monitoring at runtime, implementing ISO/TS 15066 PFL force limits, triggering gait interrupts on patient proximity, or managing safe-state transitions appropriate for a clinical environment; (2) clinical workflow integration — no open stack connects humanoid robot actions to hospital EHR systems, clinical decision support, or nursing workflow management, meaning a humanoid cannot be supervised within standard clinical tooling; (3) an open clinical demonstration dataset — every major VLA training corpus (Open X-Embodiment, ALOHA demonstrations, LeRobotDataset contributions) consists of household and tabletop manipulation tasks, with no publicly available demonstrations in clinical or clinical-simulated environments; and (4) a validated medical simulation environment — neither Genesis nor IsaacLab provides environments modeling deformable tissue, clinical instrument physics, or patient anatomy at the fidelity required to train and validate clinical manipulation policies.

The AI validation gap extends to deployment readiness. VLA models achieve high success rates on standard benchmarks — tabletop rearrangement, household tasks, ALOHA bimanual manipulation — but these benchmarks share no tasks with the clinical procedure requirements documented in \cite{atar2025humanoids}. The assumption that policies trained on tabletop datasets generalize to IV catheter insertion, endoscope navigation through anatomical cavities, or bag-valve mask ventilation is empirically untested. The distribution shift involves not only visual and geometric differences but also contact physics, tool compliance, and procedural sequencing requirements that are categorically distinct from household manipulation. Additionally, no humanoid software stack has addressed IEC 62443 Security Level 2 requirements — the minimum threshold for networked systems operating in hospital environments — leaving the teleoperation command channels, sensor streaming, and AI inference pipelines of clinical humanoid systems as an unanalyzed attack surface.
