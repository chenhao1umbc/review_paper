## 4. Medical Applications and Research

Despite growing interest in humanoid robots for healthcare, the peer-reviewed evidence base as of early 2026 is remarkably sparse. A systematic search spanning arXiv, PubMed, IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library, and Google Scholar, covering the period 2022–2026, yielded 14 qualifying papers under the bipedal-or-human-form humanoid scope defined in Section 2. The literature is strongly concentrated on a single commercial platform — the Unitree G1 — and is dominated by a single research cluster, namely the UC San Diego Yip Laboratory and Johns Hopkins University, which together account for all three papers in the clinical procedures domain. This concentration is itself a finding: the evidence for humanoid robot capability in clinical settings is real but narrow, and independent replication on alternative platforms has not yet occurred. The subsections below report the evidence as it stands, organized by clinical domain.

---

### 4.1 Clinical Procedures and Surgical Assistance

The most substantive cluster of evidence in this review concerns teleoperated humanoid robots performing discrete clinical procedures. Three papers constitute this cluster, all of which were published between 2025 and 2026 and all of which use the Unitree G1 as the experimental platform.

**Bimanual teleoperation for dexterous medical interventions.** The principal reference for humanoid capability in clinical procedures is Atar et al. \cite{atar2025humanoids}, from the UC San Diego Yip Laboratory (PI: Michael Yip). The authors developed a bimanual teleoperation system integrating the Unitree G1 with Inspire Gen4 dexterous hands — four-fingered grippers with 12 degrees of freedom (DOF) each — controlled via bilateral impedance control and real-time motion retargeting from operator hand kinematics to robot end-effectors. The system was evaluated across seven distinct clinical procedures spanning physical examination and emergency intervention: (1) cardiac and pulmonary auscultation via stethoscope placement, (2) respiratory assistance via bag-valve mask ventilation, (3) Leopold maneuvers for fetal position assessment, (4) otoscopic examination, (5) intravenous catheter insertion, (6) point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS), and (7) blood glucose finger-stick testing. Non-clinician operators achieved an aggregate procedural success rate of approximately 70% in controlled laboratory conditions using a clinical simulator and mannequin; no human subjects were involved. Auscultation and ultrasound imaging yielded the strongest performance, both tasks requiring spatial positioning of a sensor with moderate force tolerance. IV catheter insertion and finger-stick testing were the most challenging, with failures attributable specifically to force precision requirements beyond the system's current capability.

The paper explicitly documents two technical barriers to clinical translation: insufficient force output in the current actuator configuration, which precludes tasks demanding concentrated grip force, and sensor sensitivity limitations in the tactile feedback loop, which prevented adequate discrimination of tissue contact for needle-based procedures. The study is assessed at Technology Readiness Level (TRL) 4 — technology validated in laboratory conditions using representative test fixtures, but not yet in a clinical environment or with patient contact.

**Laparoscopic surgery via humanoid teleoperation.** Liang et al. \cite{liang2025lapsurgie} from the same UCSD Yip Laboratory extend humanoid clinical teleoperation to laparoscopic surgery, a substantially more demanding manipulation domain. Their LapSurgie framework introduces an inverse-mapping strategy that adapts the Unitree G1's arm kinematics to drive manual-wristed laparoscopic instruments while enforcing a remote-center-of-motion (RCM) constraint at the trocar site — a non-negotiable geometric safety requirement in all abdominal laparoscopy, ensuring the instrument pivots about the entry point rather than exerting lateral forces on the abdominal wall. The framework was validated through user studies, and the authors advance an access-equity argument: the humanoid form factor, with two anthropomorphic arms positioned at human working height, maps naturally to the ergonomic conventions of laparoscopic surgery in ways that conventional robotic surgical systems do not, potentially enabling specialist surgical capability in geographically underserved settings.

**Humanoid robot as surgical first assistant.** The most clinically significant result in this review is reported by Cho et al. \cite{cho2026humanoid} from Johns Hopkins University. In this proof-of-concept study, a teleoperated Unitree G1 served as first assistant in an actual surgical procedure — a bilateral cadaveric sphenoidectomy (endoscopic sinus surgery) performed by an attending otolaryngologist. The robot held and repositioned an endoscope under the surgeon's direction via teleoperation, providing the continuous endoscopic visualization that a human first assistant would normally supply. This is, to the authors' and this review's knowledge, the first documented use of a modern commercial bipedal humanoid in a real surgical procedural context, even though the setting was a cadaver laboratory and no living patients were involved. Cadaver studies are a recognized pre-clinical methodology in surgical research and represent the step immediately preceding institutional review board protocols with human subjects. The paper documents specific engineering targets required before clinical translation — endoscope stability, compliant force control during contact with anatomical structures, and reliable autonomous scope positioning — and identifies autonomous diagnostic scoping as the most tractable near-term capability for a humanoid first assistant.

**Platform concentration as a structural limitation.** All three papers in this subsection use the Unitree G1; P1 and P2 originate from a single laboratory at a single institution under a single principal investigator. The entire peer-reviewed evidence base for humanoid robot capability in clinical procedures therefore rests on one commercial platform and is primarily the work of one research group. While the quality of the work is high and the diversity of tasks evaluated is meaningful, independent replication on other platforms — whether academic systems such as RHP Friends or other commercial bipedal robots — has not yet occurred. This is a clear limitation of the current evidence base that must be resolved before the field can draw generalizable conclusions about humanoid clinical performance.

### 4.2 Elderly and Nursing Care

**Purpose-built nursing humanoid.** The most directly relevant demonstration of a bipedal humanoid in a nursing context is provided by Benallegue et al. \cite{benallegue2025rhp}, documenting the RHP Friends robot developed at the CNRS-AIST Joint Robotics Laboratory (JRL). RHP Friends is the only bipedal humanoid platform in the literature designed with nursing assistance as its explicit primary application domain. The system's defining architectural contribution is a seamless, mode-transparent switching mechanism between autonomous and teleoperated control: routine, repeatable tasks — navigation to a patient bedside, object retrieval — execute autonomously using onboard locomanipulation and object detection pipelines, while non-routine or contextually ambiguous tasks are handed off to a remote human operator without observable interruption from the patient's perspective. This hybrid architecture directly addresses a clinical reality: full autonomy for patient-contact tasks is neither currently safe nor validated, and mandating full teleoperation imposes prohibitive operator labor costs. The system was demonstrated over three days at the 2023 International Robot Exhibition (IREX) in Tokyo, performing patient transfer assistance (autonomous mode, using a mannequin) and circuit breaker operation as an exemplar of unanticipated task demands (teleoperated mode). No patients were involved; IREX is an exhibition event, not a clinical facility. The demonstration nonetheless constitutes the most direct bipedal humanoid nursing task performance documented in the literature. TRL assessment: 4 for the demonstrated hardware capability in a structured exhibition setting.

**Large-scale deployment evidence from a wheeled platform.** The largest real-world deployment of any humanoid-form care robot in a clinical facility found in this review is the SPRING project \cite{alamedapineda2024spring}. *Scope caveat*: the SPRING project used the ARI robot by PAL Robotics, which operates on a wheeled differential-drive base and is not bipedal; it does not qualify as a bipedal humanoid under this paper's scope definition and must not be cited as evidence for bipedal humanoid deployment. It is included here because the deployment data illuminate acceptance dynamics that bipedal systems will ultimately face in the same environments. Over a four-year EU H2020 program (2020–2024), ARI was deployed in a Parisian geriatric day-care facility with more than 60 older adult participants across multiple sessions. Acceptability was assessed using the Assistive Experience Scale (AES) and usability with the System Usability Scale (SUS); results indicated broadly positive user reception when the robot's perceptual capabilities — speech recognition, face recognition, spatial navigation in crowded spaces — performed reliably. Participants specifically cited upright posture and eye-level conversational positioning as important to dignified interaction, a finding that directly supports the form-factor rationale articulated in Section 2.2. The SPRING project represents the threshold of operational robustness and user acceptance that fully bipedal systems must replicate and surpass. For a bipedal equivalent, the relevant TRL benchmark would be TRL 5 (validated in a relevant environment), but this level has not yet been demonstrated on any bipedal platform.

**Institutional stakeholder perspectives.** Imtiaz and Khan \cite{imtiaz2024perceptions} provide the only stakeholder-perspective data in this review, surveying 269 nursing home and long-term care administrators across the United States on the prospect of humanoid robot deployment in their facilities. Administrators acknowledged potential value in resident engagement and staff support, particularly for routine, labor-intensive tasks. Primary barriers cited were cost, perceived risk of reduced human contact with residents, and the absence of demonstrated clinical effectiveness data. This survey finding is significant for deployment planning: even if technical barriers are resolved, adoption will require engagement with institutional decision-makers whose objections are economic, ethical, and evidence-based rather than technical. The absence of clinical effectiveness data — documented here across the full literature — is precisely the gap that this review's clinical domain subsections reflect.

**User-centered design methodology.** Ghosh et al. \cite{ghosh2024usercentered} report a user-centered design study combining a NAO humanoid with non-immersive virtual reality for non-pharmacological apathy management in long-term care residents, involving 14 participants across four interactive activities. *Scope caveat*: NAO is a small-form platform (58 cm) and is not a full-size bipedal humanoid; the physical interaction evidence from this study does not generalize to full-scale systems. The study's contribution to this review lies in its methodology — iterative co-design with care staff and residents — and in the finding that caregiver usability improved across design cycles, demonstrating that clinical staff can be meaningfully engaged as design partners rather than passive end-users. The co-design process documented here is a transferable approach for future bipedal humanoid deployment programs.

### 4.3 Rehabilitation

**Neuroscientific basis for humanoid-specific rehabilitation.** Nguyen, Anand, and Johnson \cite{nguyen2024eeg} report a pilot EEG study measuring sensorimotor neural responses in healthy participants during observation of actions performed by a humanoid robot versus a human actor. The study's central finding is that common neural response patterns consistent with mirror neuron system activation — the neurophysiological mechanism hypothesized to underlie action observation therapy (AOT) in stroke rehabilitation — are elicited by humanoid robot observation at levels comparable to human observation. The humanoid form factor is theorized as the specific mechanistic basis for this effect: non-humanoid robotic arms or abstract visual stimuli do not produce equivalent mirror neuron engagement, making the anthropomorphic body configuration of the humanoid robot directly relevant to its therapeutic mechanism, not merely to user preference. This is a foundational result for the use of humanoid robots as movement demonstrators in AOT programs for stroke patients. The study carries important caveats: it is a pilot study in healthy subjects, and clinical replication in patients with motor impairment following stroke is necessary before therapeutic claims can be substantiated. TRL: 3 (experimental proof of concept in controlled laboratory conditions).

**Remote neuromotor rehabilitation via humanoid telepresence.** Sobrepera et al. \cite{sobrepera2022feasibility}, the earliest qualifying paper in this review (2022), describe a Social Robot Augmented Telepresence (SRAT) system combining a humanoid robot — providing head, arms, and facial expression — with mobile telepresence to enable remote neuromotor rehabilitation by geographically distant clinicians. A case series of six participants was reported: three adult stroke survivors and three pediatric typically-developing participants. Five of six rated the SRAT system superior to standard video telepresence for therapeutic interaction, attributing the preference to the physical embodiment of the humanoid component — the ability to physically demonstrate movement, provide spatially grounded feedback, and convey nonverbal cues through body posture. TRL: 4, demonstrated in a small case series with clinical participants, though sample size precludes efficacy conclusions.

**Compliance for physical assistance tasks.** Lu et al. \cite{lu2025gentlehumanoid} report the GentleHumanoid framework, a spring-based unified compliance architecture for the Unitree G1 enabling safe physical contact during upper-body interactions including gentle hugging, sit-to-stand assistance, and object manipulation. The study is an engineering paper, not a clinical trial; no patient subjects were involved. Its relevance to rehabilitation is direct: sit-to-stand assistance is among the highest-impact activities of daily living (ADL) for fall prevention and post-operative recovery, and the framework's quantified reduction in peak contact forces relative to rigid control constitutes a concrete step toward the force limits specified by ISO/TS 15066 for physical human-robot contact in collaborative settings. This work is the only paper in the rehabilitation subsection that directly addresses the safety engineering required for physical patient assistance.

### 4.4 Mental Health and Social Interaction

**Autonomous humanoid delivery of wellbeing intervention.** Robinson et al. \cite{robinson2023brief} report a pilot randomized controlled trial (n = 230) in which an autonomous humanoid social robot delivered a brief mindful breathing training session to a subclinical general population. A recruitment uptake rate of 53% — meaning more than half of individuals approached agreed to participate — indicates the intervention was not aversive, an important prerequisite for any robot-delivered health program. Participants rated the experience as enjoyable and moderately useful. Methodologically, this is the strongest study design in this subsection: a randomized controlled trial. *Caveat*: participants were a subclinical general population, not a diagnosed clinical group; the robot model was not disclosed by the authors; and the primary measure was self-reported experience rather than a validated clinical outcome. TRL: 4 for wellbeing applications in non-clinical settings.

**Embodiment advantage in mental health interactions.** Sayis and Gunes \cite{sayis2024technology} report a comparative study (n = 42 university students) of humanoid robot versus voice assistant for therapeutic journal writing. Only the humanoid robot condition produced statistically significant mood improvements, increased self-disclosure over time, and positive change in perception of the interaction across the study period. The voice assistant condition produced none of these effects. This is direct comparative evidence that physical embodiment — specifically the presence of an anthropomorphic robotic body — matters for mental health interactions in ways that purely auditory interfaces do not, supporting what the authors term the embodiment advantage hypothesis. *Caveat*: the population was university students in an educational wellness context, not patients with a diagnosed mental health condition; the robot model was not specified. The embodiment advantage finding is suggestive and merits replication in clinical populations.

**LLM integration for personalized dementia care.** Yuan et al. \cite{yuan2025integrating} propose a framework combining a humanoid robot, reinforcement learning, and large language models for adaptive personalized dementia care. The framework introduces probabilistic cognitive-emotional state modeling and LLM-based behavior simulation to address the data scarcity problem inherent in training adaptive caregiving agents for dementia patients, where reliable behavioral data is difficult to collect at scale. *Scope caveat*: this paper uses the Pepper robot, which operates on a wheeled base and does not meet the bipedal humanoid scope definition of this paper. It is cited here exclusively for the LLM integration paradigm — the cognitive-emotional state modeling approach and the use of LLMs to simulate and personalize behavior — which is directly applicable to bipedal platforms. The framework represents the most sophisticated AI integration design in the elderly and mental health care literature covered by this review.

### 4.5 Hospital Logistics

No qualifying papers were found in the 2022–2026 search window for humanoid-specific hospital logistics tasks — medication delivery, supply transport, specimen handling, or patient transport — performed by bipedal humanoid robots.

This absence is notable because hospital logistics is frequently cited in industry and policy literature as one of the most tractable near-term applications for humanoid robots in healthcare, requiring ambulatory locomotion and basic manipulation but not the precision, sterile technique, or safety validation levels demanded by clinical procedures. The evidence gap is plausibly explained by two structural factors. First, wheeled autonomous mobile robots — including the TUG (Aethon/Swisslog), Moxi (Diligent Robotics), and Keenon delivery platforms — already address hospital logistics in major health systems with proven operational reliability; the humanoid form factor provides no demonstrated functional advantage over these cheaper, faster, and already-certified alternatives for this task category. Second, humanoid hardware development in 2022–2026 was concentrated on high-value industrial logistics contexts (warehouse automation, automotive assembly), with academic research attention directed toward the more scientifically novel clinical procedure and HRI domains. Neither commercial nor academic programs pursued hospital logistics as a primary investigation target during the review period. TRL for bipedal humanoid hospital logistics: 2 (technology concept formulated; no demonstrated examples in relevant operational contexts).

**TRL scale**: ISO 16290:2013 adapted for clinical robotics. TRL 1 = basic principles observed; TRL 2 = technology concept formulated; TRL 3 = experimental proof of concept; TRL 4 = technology validated in laboratory; TRL 5 = technology validated in relevant environment; TRL 7 = system prototype demonstrated in operational environment; TRL 9 = system proven in operational environment.

The aggregate picture across all five domains is consistent: the field is at TRL 3–4 for bipedal humanoid clinical tasks. Feasibility has been demonstrated in controlled laboratory and pre-clinical settings, primarily by two research groups on a single platform, but no clinical trial with patient subjects, no regulatory submission, and no institutional deployment exists for any full-size bipedal humanoid robot as of early 2026. The clinical procedures cluster (Section 4.1) provides the strongest evidence of functional capability; the elderly care cluster (Section 4.2) provides the strongest evidence of deployment-context realism. The rehabilitation, mental health, and logistics domains remain earlier in the development pipeline. The technical and regulatory barriers that explain this gap are the subject of Section 5.
