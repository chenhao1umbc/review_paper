## 2. Background

This section establishes the contextual foundations for the survey. We describe the healthcare imperative that motivates increased automation (Section 2.1), explain why the humanoid form factor is particularly relevant to clinical environments (Section 2.2), characterize the inflection point created by large language and vision-language-action models (Section 2.3), trace the historical trajectory of humanoid robotics from locomotion research to commercial scale-up (Section 2.4), and situate humanoid robots relative to the existing clinical robot landscape (Section 2.5).

---

### 2.1 The Healthcare Imperative

The global healthcare workforce is under structural strain that technology alone cannot resolve, but that technology may help mitigate. The World Health Organization has widely reported a projected shortage of approximately 10 million healthcare workers by 2030, concentrated primarily in low- and middle-income countries but with significant pressure also on high-income systems where demographic aging compounds absolute workforce insufficiency \cite{atar2025humanoids}. The root cause is well understood: populations in Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) countries are aging faster than working-age cohorts are growing. In Japan — the most extreme case — persons aged 65 and over already represent approximately 30% of the total population, a proportion that continues to rise under below-replacement fertility rates. The METI 2023 robotics roadmap explicitly identifies nursing-capable robots as a national strategic target by 2030, reflecting governmental acknowledgment that the eldercare workforce gap is structurally unsolvable by recruitment alone.

The care gap operates at two intersecting levels. First, the absolute number of care hours demanded rises with the prevalence of chronic disease, multimorbidity, and frailty in an older population: an individual with dementia and comorbid heart failure requires qualitatively more nursing time than a healthy adult, and the number of such individuals is growing rapidly. Second, the tasks that constitute professional care — physical repositioning of patients, vital sign monitoring, medication administration, rehabilitation exercise guidance, and social engagement — are labor-intensive and resist substitution by currently deployed information technology. Electronic health record systems and remote monitoring platforms reduce administrative overhead but do not reduce the need for physical presence at the bedside.

Automation is increasingly discussed as a necessary complement — not a replacement — for human care. The framing matters: the goal is not to displace nurses or physicians, but to extend their effective reach by delegating tasks that are physically demanding, repetitive, or logistically routine, freeing trained clinicians for higher-order judgment and human connection. Wheeled mobile robots (Section 2.5.4) already handle medication transport and specimen delivery in hundreds of hospitals. Exoskeletons (Section 2.5.3) assist rehabilitation and reduce caregiver musculoskeletal strain. Social robots (Section 2.5.1) provide cognitive stimulation and companionship in dementia care settings. Each of these automation layers is real and documented. What remains largely unrealized is a robot capable of performing the physical care tasks — patient repositioning, standing assist, equipment handling, clinical examination — that currently require a human body in proximity to the patient.

This is where the humanoid form factor becomes relevant: a robot that can operate in the existing physical infrastructure of hospitals and care homes — designed for human-sized, bipedal operators — without requiring facility modification.

### 2.2 Humanoid Form Factor: Rationale for Healthcare

For the purposes of this survey, a *humanoid robot* is defined as a bipedal, free-standing robot with a human-proportioned morphology: head, torso, two arms, and two legs, typically ranging from 150 to 200 cm in height. This definition explicitly excludes social robots such as Pepper (120 cm, wheeled base) and NAO (58 cm, child-scale), surgical manipulator arms such as the da Vinci system (stationary, single-purpose), and wearable exoskeletons. The rationale for this scope restriction is not hierarchical — social robots and surgical robots are clinically important and are discussed in Section 2.5 — but conceptual: the form-factor hypothesis is specifically about whether human-scale bipedal morphology confers advantages in human-built environments that smaller or non-mobile systems cannot provide.

Three distinct lines of argument support the hypothesis that the humanoid form factor is particularly promising for healthcare deployment:

**Environmental compatibility.** Hospitals, care homes, and residences are built for human-sized bipedal operators. Doorways, corridors, stairways, beds, examination tables, supply cabinets, and clinical instruments are all dimensioned for a person approximately 160–180 cm tall with two-handed reach. A bipedal humanoid can, in principle, navigate these environments without infrastructure modification: walk through standard doorways, climb stairs to reach a room not served by freight elevator, reach across a standard hospital bed, and operate existing clinical tools (stethoscope, bag-valve mask, otoscope, ultrasound probe) designed for human hands. The technical demonstration by Atar et al. \cite{atar2025humanoids} explicitly grounds their work in this observation: the Unitree G1, operating in a standard clinical simulator room, demonstrated seven medical procedures (auscultation, respiratory assistance, fetal position assessment, otoscopic examination, IV catheter insertion, ultrasound-guided examination, and glucose finger-stick testing) using existing clinical instruments, not purpose-built robot interfaces. No facility was modified for the robot.

**Neuroscientific basis for human-robot interaction.** A second argument for the humanoid form factor is neurological rather than mechanical. Action observation therapy (AOT) — a rehabilitation technique in which patients observe biological motion to activate sensorimotor circuits — rests on the mirror neuron hypothesis: observing purposive action activates similar neural circuits as performing that action. The functional efficacy of AOT for stroke rehabilitation depends critically on whether the observed agent is sufficiently biological in appearance to engage these circuits. Nguyen et al. \cite{nguyen2024eeg} provide the most direct empirical evidence available: EEG recordings from healthy participants during observation of a humanoid robot performing reaching and grasping actions showed sensorimotor response patterns comparable to those elicited by observing a human agent perform the same actions. This finding — while from a pilot study requiring replication in larger and patient-specific populations — provides neurophysiological grounding for the hypothesis that the humanoid form specifically (rather than any mechanical demonstrator) may be required for AOT efficacy. It also implies that the appearance design of a rehabilitation humanoid is not aesthetically arbitrary but has functional consequences for therapeutic outcome.

A complementary behavioral finding comes from Sayis and Gunes \cite{sayis2024technology}, who conducted a controlled comparison (n=42) in which participants engaged in therapeutic journal writing with either a humanoid robot or a voice-only assistant over multiple sessions. Only the humanoid robot condition produced improvements in mood, increased self-disclosure across sessions, and positive perception change over time. The voice-only condition did not. The authors attribute the difference to the physical embodiment of the humanoid — its capacity for gaze, gesture, and postural cues that a disembodied voice cannot provide. This study used an educational/wellness population rather than a clinical one, and the specific humanoid model is not disclosed; the finding should be treated as suggestive rather than definitive. Nevertheless, it points to embodiment as a functional variable in mental health interactions, not a cosmetic one.

**User acceptance and eye-level interaction.** A third argument concerns the social phenomenology of care. Patients in clinical settings are frequently in positions of physical vulnerability: lying in bed, seated in wheelchairs, or otherwise at a lower eye level than standing caregivers. Interaction with a robot at adult standing height — with eye contact at approximately the user's face level when both are seated — may be qualitatively different from interaction with a small robot or a floor-mounted system. The SPRING project, which deployed the ARI robot (PAL Robotics) — a wheeled platform with a human-form upper body, height approximately 165 cm — in a real geriatric day-care facility in Paris with over 60 older adult participants, found that end-users explicitly cited the upright, eye-level conversational presence of the robot as important for dignified interaction \cite{alamedapineda2024spring}. It should be noted explicitly that ARI is a wheeled platform and does not meet the bipedal humanoid definition used in this survey; therefore the SPRING findings cannot be cited as evidence for bipedal humanoid deployment specifically. However, the user experience finding — that human-scale, eye-level presence matters for perceived dignity in elder care HRI — is directly relevant to the form-factor argument, regardless of the mobility substrate.

Additional contextual evidence comes from research on the HRP-4C platform, which features a female appearance with articulated facial expressions. Studies of HRP-4C demonstrated that gait naturalness and facial expression generation systematically affected observer ratings of the robot's approachability and social presence, providing further evidence that human-like appearance affects interaction quality in ways that may be clinically relevant \cite{cao2025humanoid}. These findings collectively support — but do not yet conclusively prove — the form-factor hypothesis at clinical scale. The evidence to date is encouraging; it is not sufficient to substitute for clinical validation.

### 2.3 The LLM/VLM/VLA Inflection Point

Prior to approximately 2022, humanoid robots required explicit, hand-crafted programming for every behavioral task. A robot capable of performing a stethoscope placement procedure had to be programmed, step by step, for that specific procedure — its movements, sensor queries, and conditional responses specified in advance by an engineer. Deploying the same robot for a second, slightly different procedure required a substantially new programming effort. This paradigm fundamentally limited humanoid deployment in clinical environments, which are intrinsically unstructured: patient bodies differ, room layouts change, equipment is not always where it was last time, and care needs are not scripted.

The emergence of large language models (LLMs), vision-language models (VLMs), and vision-language-action (VLA) models has materially changed this constraint. GPT-4 and its successors (GPT-4o, o1) demonstrated that natural language can serve as a general-purpose task specification interface, capable of interpreting novel instructions, decomposing them into action plans, and recovering from unexpected situations through dialogue. Gemini 2.0 (Google DeepMind) extended this to real-time multimodal reasoning. Physical Intelligence's \(\pi_0\) and \(\pi_0.5\) models introduced flow-based VLA architectures that map language instructions and visual observations directly to robot actions, trained on thousands of hours of real robot demonstrations across diverse manipulation tasks. OpenVLA demonstrated that open-source, community-accessible VLA models could achieve meaningful generalization from limited task demonstrations — enabling fine-tuning for novel tasks without full retraining.

The implications for humanoid robots in clinical settings are direct. A humanoid robot integrated with a capable VLM can, in principle, receive a natural language instruction ("check the IV line for the patient in bed three"), interpret its environmental context from vision, and compose a sequence of actions to execute the task — without explicit per-task programming. Failure recovery becomes possible through language-mediated clarification ("I couldn't reach the IV bag; the pole is too tall — what should I do?"). Task generalization across similar but non-identical situations becomes feasible through the LLM's contextual reasoning capacity.

Yuan et al. \cite{yuan2025integrating} demonstrated this integration paradigm in a dementia care context, combining reinforcement learning with a large language model to generate adaptive, personalized robot behavior for a Pepper platform. Although Pepper is a wheeled robot (not within our bipedal scope), the LLM integration framework — and specifically its ability to model patient-specific cognitive-emotional states and adapt behavior accordingly — is directly applicable to bipedal humanoid platforms. The paradigm, not the platform, is the relevant contribution here.

The open-source ecosystem reflects the community's recognition that this infrastructure is foundational. The LeRobot framework (HuggingFace; 22,179 GitHub stars as of March 2026) provides a standardized, hardware-agnostic pipeline for training imitation learning and VLA policies on humanoid hardware, including explicit support for the Unitree G1. The openpi repository (Physical Intelligence; 10,551 stars) releases \(\pi_0\) checkpoints and fine-tuning infrastructure under Apache 2.0, enabling academic and healthcare research groups to adapt foundation model policies for novel tasks. These star counts are cited not as quality metrics but as indicators of community engagement: at this scale, these repositories represent infrastructure, not experiments.

The commercial humanoid wave of 2022–2026 — the thirteen systems catalogued in Section 3 — coincides with this LLM inflection, and the coincidence is not accidental. Twelve of the thirteen commercial humanoid systems documented in Section 3 have confirmed or announced LLM/VLA integration as of 2026. The design assumption of current commercial humanoids is language-conditioned, generalist behavior; these systems are not being built as specialized single-task machines. This architectural choice is directly relevant to clinical deployment: a humanoid designed for language-conditioned generalism is, at least in principle, capable of adapting to the task diversity of clinical environments in a way that earlier, fixed-program humanoids were not.

### 2.4 Historical Evolution of Humanoid Robotics

The history of humanoid robotics over the past three decades can be usefully organized into three eras distinguished by their primary technical achievements, followed by the nascent healthcare entry period that this review covers.

**Locomotion proof-of-concept era (1996–2010).** The central technical challenge of this period was stable bipedal walking. Honda's P-series prototypes, culminating in ASIMO (2000), demonstrated that dynamic bipedal walking — including stair climbing, changes in direction, and running at 9 km/h — was achievable with then-current actuation and control technology \cite{cao2025humanoid}. The AIST HRP series (HRP-2 through HRP-4, 1998–2010) provided a parallel lineage of platforms that established the research infrastructure for whole-body motion planning and served as reference platforms for dozens of international research groups. These systems proved that bipedal locomotion was a solvable engineering problem. However, they had no meaningful manipulation capability, no intelligence, and no path to clinical relevance. ASIMO's healthcare contribution was indirect: the locomotion research generated spin-off wearable walking-assist devices (Stride Management Assist, Bodyweight Support Assist). ASIMO itself was retired in 2022.

**Manipulation and integration era (2010–2020).** The DARPA Robotics Challenge (DRC, 2012–2015) defined the central problem of this era: could a humanoid perform a coordinated set of manipulation and mobility tasks in a disaster-response scenario — driving a vehicle, turning a valve, opening a door, traversing debris? The DRC Trials and Finals revealed, in vivid public demonstration, the gap between locomotion capability (teams had solved walking) and robust task execution (most teams struggled significantly with manipulation tasks under time pressure). Boston Dynamics' hydraulic Atlas became the reference platform for whole-body manipulation research and produced spectacular locomotion demonstrations (backflips, parkour) that demonstrated dynamic capability while revealing that force-controlled manipulation at clinical precision remained largely unsolved \cite{cao2025humanoid}. The decade also saw the consolidation of learning-based approaches to locomotion control, particularly deep reinforcement learning policies trained in simulation and transferred to hardware.

**Commercial scale-up era (2022–present).** The confluence of electric actuator maturation (quasi-direct drive motors replacing hydraulics), learning-based locomotion policies, and LLM integration produced the current commercial humanoid wave. Figure AI (Figure 02, 168 cm, 20–25 kg payload), Tesla (Optimus Gen 2, 173 cm), Boston Dynamics (electric Atlas, ~150–175 cm, 30–50 kg payload), Agility Robotics (Digit v4, 175 cm), Unitree (G1 and H1), 1X Technologies (NEO Beta), Apptronik (Apollo), Sanctuary AI (Phoenix Gen 8), Fourier Intelligence (GR-2), UBTECH (Walker X), AgiBot (A2), and NEURA Robotics (4NE-1) — thirteen distinct commercial humanoid systems — reached hardware maturity and initial commercial deployment between 2022 and 2026. Global investment in humanoid robotics grew rapidly through 2024, driven by the convergence of electric actuator maturation and LLM integration, with confirmed commercial deployments concentrated in automotive and logistics applications. As of March 2026, none of these thirteen systems has been deployed in a confirmed clinical patient care context, a finding that is itself central to this review's contribution.

**Healthcare entry point (2024–2026).** The first documented research using modern commercial humanoids for actual clinical tasks falls within this period. Atar et al. \cite{atar2025humanoids} demonstrated bimanual teleoperation of a Unitree G1 across seven clinical procedures with approximately 70% aggregate success in a controlled laboratory setting — the first systematic evaluation of a current-generation commercial humanoid against a clinically meaningful task battery. Cho et al. \cite{cho2026humanoid} extended this to a cadaveric endoscopic sinus surgery at Johns Hopkins University, with a teleoperated Unitree G1 serving as first assistant providing endoscopic visualization during a bilateral sphenoidectomy — the first documented use of a modern commercial bipedal humanoid in an actual (if cadaveric) surgical procedure. This survey covers this nascent period and aims to characterize both what has been achieved and what technical, regulatory, and clinical translation work remains.

### 2.5 Humanoid Robots vs. Non-Humanoid Alternatives in Healthcare

A principled comparison with existing clinical robot categories is necessary to locate the humanoid value proposition precisely. Each established category represents real clinical deployment and a real evidence base; the question is what each category can and cannot do, and whether humanoids offer something qualitatively different rather than merely quantitatively better.

#### 2.5.1 Social Robots: NAO, Pepper, PARO

Social robots have the deepest clinical evidence base in robotics. NAO (SoftBank/Aldebaran, 58 cm, 25 DOF) has accumulated over 1,000 peer-reviewed publications, with clinical use spanning autism spectrum disorder therapy, elderly care, psychiatric HRI, and hospital patient reception. Randomized controlled trials using NAO for ASD social skills development have been completed with up to 70 participants (DREAM, NCT03323931). Pepper (SoftBank/Aldebaran, 120 cm, wheeled) has been deployed in approximately 2,000 Japanese healthcare facilities for dementia cognitive stimulation, companionship, hospital check-in assistance, and appointment reminder delivery \cite{sorensen2025humanoid, cunha2025humanoid}. PARO, a seal-form therapeutic robot, has an established evidence base for agitation and loneliness reduction in dementia care.

These platforms set the deployment high-water mark for robots in clinical care settings. The clinical and institutional acceptance they have achieved — regulatory, procedural, cultural — is real and should not be undervalued. But their form-factor constraints are also real. NAO at 58 cm cannot reach adult-height work surfaces, operate standard clinical equipment, or perform physical care tasks for adults. Pepper at 120 cm with a wheeled base cannot navigate stairways, use standard clinical beds, or manipulate objects at adult-height work surfaces effectively; its production was paused in 2021. PARO is a single-purpose therapeutic device with no manipulation capability whatsoever.

The argument for humanoids builds on, rather than dismisses, the social robot evidence base: these platforms have demonstrated that robot-mediated social and cognitive support in healthcare is feasible and accepted at scale. They have also revealed the ceiling of what non-physical interaction can achieve. Bipedal humanoids must justify their substantially higher cost and engineering complexity by enabling the physical care task layer that social robots cannot.

#### 2.5.2 Surgical Robots: da Vinci, Versius, Hugo

Robotic-assisted surgery is the most established and commercially dominant category of clinical robots. The da Vinci Surgical System (Intuitive Surgical) is FDA-cleared (Class II, 510(k)) and CE-marked, with over 10 million procedures completed across urology, gynecology, general surgery, and thoracic surgery. CMR Surgical's Versius and Medtronic's Hugo represent the second generation of CE-marked laparoscopic surgical platforms, offering modular architectures at lower capital cost than da Vinci \cite{morgan2022robots}.

Surgical robots are not competitors to humanoid clinical assistants; they are complementary systems addressing a fundamentally different need. Da Vinci and its peers are stationary, surgeon-controlled, single-purpose systems requiring a complete operating room setup and highly trained operators. They cannot be repurposed for patient transport, repositioning, vital sign monitoring, or eldercare. A humanoid clinical assistant operating in a post-surgical ward is not competing with da Vinci — it is addressing the nursing and logistics tasks that da Vinci cannot. The distinction is between specialized, stationary, surgeon-controlled precision instruments and general-purpose, ambulatory, semi-autonomous assistants.

#### 2.5.3 Exoskeletons: ReWalk, HAL, Ekso Bionics

Wearable robotic exoskeletons provide a well-established and FDA-cleared category of clinical hardware for rehabilitation and mobility assistance. ReWalk P6.0 (ReWalk Robotics) and Ekso GT (Ekso Bionics) are FDA-cleared (Class II) for spinal cord injury rehabilitation; Cyberdyne's HAL has Japanese MHLW regulatory approval and CE marking for neurological and orthopedic rehabilitation. These systems have strong clinical evidence bases for their approved indications \cite{sorensen2025humanoid}.

Exoskeletons and humanoid clinical assistants are complementary, not substitutable. An exoskeleton assists the patient's own movement — it is worn by the patient, requires the patient to be ambulatory (or near-ambulatory), and involves the patient's musculoskeletal system as the primary actuator. A humanoid provides external physical assistance without patient-worn hardware — it can lift, reposition, or support a patient who is non-ambulatory or whose limb function is severely impaired. For a patient who cannot stand unaided, an exoskeleton is not an option; assisted standing from a humanoid may be. The GentleHumanoid framework (Stanford, Unitree G1) demonstrates this potential: learning upper-body compliance for sit-to-stand assistance reduced peak contact forces relative to rigid control, addressing one of the most clinically impactful activities of daily living for fall prevention and post-operative recovery \cite{lu2025gentlehumanoid}.

#### 2.5.4 Wheeled Mobile Robots: TUG, Moxi

Autonomous wheeled mobile robots for hospital logistics represent the category with the largest confirmed clinical deployment footprint. TUG (Aethon/ST Logistics) robots are deployed in hundreds of US hospitals for automated medication delivery, supply transport, and specimen transport along pre-mapped pathways. Moxi (Diligent Robotics) performs "runner" logistics tasks — retrieving supplies from storerooms and delivering them to nursing stations — and has been deployed at major US health systems including Texas Health Resources and Houston Methodist. These systems have demonstrated autonomous operation in live clinical environments, navigating elevators and corridors, without disrupting workflows \cite{huang2023intelligent, morgan2022robots}.

The logistics layer these robots address is real and valuable: TUG and Moxi reduce nursing time spent on supply retrieval, a non-clinical task that nonetheless consumes meaningful percentages of nursing shifts. However, wheeled mobile logistics robots operate on fixed maps and pre-defined pathways. They cannot navigate stairs or unstructured environments. They cannot interact with clinical equipment not specifically engineered for their interfaces. They cannot perform physical patient care tasks — repositioning, lifting, assessment. They are purpose-built for one functional category: autonomous point-to-point transport on flat, pre-mapped surfaces.

The humanoid argument relative to wheeled logistics robots is a division of labor argument, not a superiority argument: wheeled mobile robots solve the logistics layer effectively and should continue to do so. Humanoids are needed for the physical assistance and care layer — the tasks that require human-like mobility across varied terrain and human-like manipulation of existing clinical tools. These are different problems requiring different architectures.

### 2.6 Section Summary

The four subsections of Section 2.5 collectively frame a finding that motivates this review: no existing clinical robot category combines (a) general ambulatory mobility in human-built environments, (b) bimanual dexterous manipulation of existing clinical tools, and (c) natural language-mediated task specification enabling adaptive behavior in unstructured settings. Social robots address (c) partially but not (a) or (b). Surgical robots address (b) for specific surgical tasks but not (a) or (c). Exoskeletons complement (b) for patient-powered rehabilitation but are not external assistants. Wheeled mobile robots address a logistics sub-function of (a) but not (b) or (c).

This combination — ambulatory general mobility, bimanual dexterous manipulation of existing tools, and language-conditioned adaptive task specification — is the humanoid value proposition for healthcare. The evidence reviewed in this section characterizes it as *particularly promising*, not as *clinically proven*: the neuroscientific basis for HRI is encouraging but requires clinical-scale validation; the environmental compatibility argument is mechanically sound but unproven at clinical operational scale; the LLM inflection point enables capability that two years ago was unavailable, but the reliability and safety margins required for clinical autonomy have not been demonstrated. The remainder of this survey characterizes where the field stands, what has been demonstrated, and what must be resolved before this promise can be realized in practice.
