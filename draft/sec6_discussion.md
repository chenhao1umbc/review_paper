## 6. Discussion and Future Outlook

The evidence surveyed in Sections 3–5 presents a field at an inflection point: capable enough to demonstrate clinical relevance in controlled settings, yet short of every threshold — safety engineering, clinical evidence, and regulatory precedent — required for operational deployment. This section synthesizes that evidence into an assessment of the field's current position, identifies the three near-term roles with the strongest case for advancement, and specifies the technical, institutional, and regulatory steps that must occur in sequence before humanoid robots can achieve meaningful clinical deployment within a five-year horizon.

---

### 6.1 Technology Readiness Assessment

The Technology Readiness Level (TRL) assignments in Section 4, Table 1 provide the starting point for this assessment. The aggregate finding — TRL 3–4 across all domains for bipedal humanoid platforms — is not a uniform statement about equivalent evidence; the TRL ceiling for each domain is governed by a specific, domain-particular barrier. Advancing any domain to TRL 5 (technology validated in a relevant environment, i.e., first human-subject pilot in a controlled clinical setting) requires clearing that barrier, not the full suite of challenges documented in Section 5.

**Clinical procedures (current TRL 3–4).** The three papers in this domain — all using the Unitree G1, all from the UCSD Yip Laboratory or Johns Hopkins \cite{atar2025humanoids,liang2025lapsurgie,cho2026humanoid} — constitute the most technically substantive evidence in the review. Atar et al. achieved 70% aggregate procedural success across seven clinical tasks with non-clinician teleoperators in a laboratory setting \cite{atar2025humanoids}; Cho et al. extended this to a cadaveric surgical setting, completing a bilateral sphenoidectomy with a humanoid first assistant \cite{cho2026humanoid}. The cadaveric study is the current frontier of the evidence sequence: it is the step immediately preceding an IRB-approved human-subject feasibility trial. Advancing to TRL 5 requires two things simultaneously. First, a structured IRB-approved feasibility study in a specific, narrow indication — telemedicine-assisted physical examination is the most defensible starting point, because fully teleoperated operation keeps a human in the decision loop at all times, substantially reducing the regulatory risk profile compared to any autonomous patient-contact task. Second, at least one independent replication on a platform other than the Unitree G1: the entire peer-reviewed evidence base for clinical procedure capability currently rests on one commercial platform from one research cluster, and findings cannot be treated as platform-generalizable until that concentration is broken.

**Elderly and nursing care (TRL 4–5 for social robots; TRL 3–4 for bipedal humanoids).** The gap between platform classes is analytically significant. The SPRING project (ARI, wheeled) achieved TRL 4–5 through real-world deployment with 60+ older adult users in a Parisian geriatric day-care facility over four years \cite{alamedapineda2024spring}. RHP Friends — the only purpose-built bipedal nursing humanoid in the literature — demonstrated patient transfer tasks at the 2023 International Robot Exhibition but has not been evaluated in an actual care facility with patient subjects \cite{benallegue2025rhp}. The path to TRL 5 for bipedal humanoids in this domain is therefore not a question of platform capability but of deployment context: the SPRING project provides both the deployment methodology and the user-acceptance evaluation framework; RHP Friends provides the platform. A structured pilot in an actual care facility, using SPRING-derived methodology on a bipedal platform, is the specific experiment that would advance this domain. The administrator survey findings of Imtiaz and Khan \cite{imtiaz2024perceptions} — which identify cost, perceived reduction in human contact, and absence of clinical effectiveness data as the primary institutional barriers — indicate that this pilot must also generate structured adoption-feasibility evidence alongside technical performance data.

**Rehabilitation (TRL 3–4).** The evidence structure here is scientifically promising but temporally fragmented. Nguyen et al. establish the neuroscientific mechanism (mirror neuron system activation during humanoid robot observation, comparable to human actor observation) that provides the theoretical basis for humanoid-specific movement demonstration in stroke rehabilitation \cite{nguyen2024eeg}. Sobrepera et al. establish clinical acceptability in a small case series (n=6, five of six rated the humanoid telepresence system superior to standard video) \cite{sobrepera2022feasibility}. Lu et al. establish the contact-safety engineering foundation, demonstrating that quasi-static upper-body forces can approach ISO/TS 15066 PFL thresholds in a sit-to-stand assistance task \cite{lu2025gentlehumanoid}. These three papers, from three independent groups, collectively build an evidence case no single paper makes alone. The path to TRL 5 is a powered IRB-approved clinical trial in stroke rehabilitation: the EEG evidence provides scientific justification for the mechanism, the SRAT case series provides acceptability evidence, and the GentleHumanoid framework provides the safety engineering foundation.

**Mental health and HRI (TRL 4–5 in non-clinical populations; TRL 3 for clinical populations).** Robinson et al. achieved an RCT design (n=230) demonstrating that humanoid-delivered wellbeing interventions are accepted and rated positively by a subclinical general population \cite{robinson2023brief}; Sayis and Gunes demonstrated that the humanoid condition — uniquely, compared to a voice assistant — produced statistically significant mood improvement and increased self-disclosure in a university student population \cite{sayis2024technology}. These are methodologically strong results that carry a critical transferability caveat: neither study involves a diagnosed patient population in an active care context. The defining barrier in this domain is not the platform or the intervention design — it is the clinical evidence gap. Advancing to TRL 5 requires a feasibility study with a specific patient population (e.g., mild dementia patients in a structured day-care program, analogous to the SPRING project scope) where a humanoid delivers a defined therapeutic interaction protocol and outcomes are assessed on validated clinical scales.

**Hospital logistics (TRL 2).** No peer-reviewed evidence exists for humanoid-specific hospital logistics tasks. The absence is structurally explained rather than accidental: wheeled autonomous mobile robots (TUG, Moxi, Keenon) already address medication delivery, supply transport, and specimen handling in major health systems with demonstrated operational reliability and existing regulatory clearances. The bipedal form factor provides no demonstrated functional advantage for these tasks, and the cost-benefit analysis is unfavorable given the maturity gap between bipedal and wheeled platforms. Hospital logistics is not a priority for bipedal humanoid development at this stage; the field's limited clinical research capacity is better directed toward the domains where the humanoid form factor provides a specific, theoretically motivated advantage.

**Cross-domain finding.** No domain has reached TRL 7 (system prototype demonstrated in operational environment). TRL 5 — the threshold of first human-subject validation in a controlled clinical setting — is the realistic five-year target for the three leading domains. This is not a pessimistic assessment; TRL 5 is the threshold that generates the clinical evidence required for regulatory engagement, and it is achievable from the current evidence base without any foundational technical breakthrough.

### 6.2 Near-Term Deployable Roles

Three roles have the strongest case for TRL 5 achievement within five years, assessed on the basis of evidence strength, barrier tractability, and regulatory risk profile.

**Role 1: Telemedicine and telepresence clinical assistant.** The teleoperated mode is the key risk-mitigation design choice: by keeping a human operator in the decision loop at all times, it avoids the regulatory classification and safety validation burdens associated with autonomous patient contact. The technical infrastructure exists: Atar et al. demonstrate teleoperated clinical task execution across seven procedures \cite{atar2025humanoids}, and Liang et al. extend this to laparoscopic surgery with the added kinematic constraint of remote-center-of-motion enforcement \cite{liang2025lapsurgie}. The Unitree xr\_teleoperate open-source stack provides the practical teleoperation infrastructure. The primary application is specialist access extension — enabling a remote specialist to conduct a physical examination or assist in a procedure in a rural or low-resource setting where specialist travel is impractical. Realizing this role at TRL 5 requires: a defined physical examination protocol, remote operator training standards, and IRB oversight, none of which require new safety standards or autonomous patient-contact validation.

**Role 2: Rehabilitation movement demonstrator.** This role requires no direct patient contact: the humanoid demonstrates movements that the patient observes or follows. The bipedal gait safety gap identified in Section 5.2 — the most universally blocking technical barrier in the review — becomes irrelevant to this specific role, because the safety concern is dynamic contact force during locomotion near patients, not movement observation at distance. The neuroscientific rationale is grounded in P5's mirror neuron evidence \cite{nguyen2024eeg}; the clinical acceptability is established by P6's case series \cite{sobrepera2022feasibility}. The regulatory precedent path for this role is the most developed of any role in this review: nine registered clinical trials involving NAO for ASD therapy and elderly care, and five involving Pepper for neuropsychological assessment and dementia care, exist in ClinicalTrials.gov as of March 2026. These trials have not used full-size bipedal platforms, but their IRB protocols and outcome measures are directly adaptable. Advancing requires a registered feasibility trial adapting a NAO or Pepper protocol to a full-size bipedal humanoid demonstrator in stroke rehabilitation.

**Role 3: Structured eldercare companion and physical assistant.** RHP Friends is the only platform designed specifically for this role, and it has demonstrated autonomous patient transfer — the highest-impact physical assistance task — in a structured exhibition context \cite{benallegue2025rhp}. The hybrid autonomous/teleoperated architecture, in which routine tasks run autonomously and non-routine tasks are handed to a remote operator without visible mode transition, is the correct design for this deployment scenario: it achieves operationally meaningful autonomy while maintaining human oversight for edge cases, directly reducing the regulatory burden. The SPRING project provides the deployment methodology and user-acceptance evaluation framework from a real clinical environment \cite{alamedapineda2024spring}. Realizing this role at TRL 5 requires an actual care facility pilot — the methodology exists; only the institutional partnership and IRB protocol remain to be established.

### 6.3 Research Priorities

Four priorities, ordered by their blocking impact on the entire field:

**Priority 1: Clinical safety validation for bipedal gait in patient-contact environments.** This is the most universally blocking gap in the review. Section 5.2 documents four gait-induced contact risk scenarios — balance corrections, stumble recovery, narrow-corridor navigation, and postural transitions — that no existing standard (ISO/TS 15066, ISO 13482, ISO 10218) was designed to address. No IRB will approve human-subject studies involving physical patient contact by a full-size bipedal humanoid without instrumented biomechanical data on these force profiles and an accepted safety standard or architectural safeguard that bounds them. This is the single most leverage-intensive investment the field can make: resolving it unblocks physical patient-contact studies in all three leading domains simultaneously.

**Priority 2: Human-subject feasibility studies in leading applications.** The cadaveric endoscopy study of Cho et al. \cite{cho2026humanoid} is the current clinical frontier. The specific next step in the evidence sequence is an IRB-approved, registered feasibility study in a narrow indication with an explicit safety monitor protocol. Academic medical centers with established humanoid research programs — UC San Diego (Yip Laboratory, responsible for P1, P2, and P3) and Johns Hopkins (Cho et al., P3) — are the natural sites. The telemedicine-assisted examination and rehabilitation movement demonstrator roles, where patient-contact safety validation is not the primary barrier, should be prioritized for the first human-subject studies to establish the clinical evidence precedent.

**Priority 3: Open clinical demonstration dataset for VLA training.** The leading VLA models — π₀, OpenVLA, LeRobot — require training data. No public dataset of humanoid robot demonstrations in clinical or clinical-simulated environments exists. Every major training corpus (Open X-Embodiment, ALOHA demonstrations, LeRobotDataset contributions) consists of household and tabletop manipulation tasks that share no procedural, geometric, or contact-physics properties with clinical tasks. Creating an open clinical demonstration dataset — even using simulators and clinical task protocols with mannequins — would unlock foundation model fine-tuning for clinical applications and enable the broader research community to advance clinical policy learning without requiring direct access to hardware or clinical facilities.

**Priority 4: Platform diversification for evidence generalizability.** The concentration of clinical procedure evidence on the Unitree G1 (P1, P2, P3) is a scientific limitation documented explicitly in Section 4.1. Replication of clinical task evaluations on RHP Friends, TALOS, or other platforms would establish whether current findings are platform-specific or generalizable to the bipedal humanoid class. This is achievable with modest funding, requires no new regulatory approvals, and directly strengthens the evidentiary basis for the field.

### 6.4 Regulatory Roadmap

The path to the first regulatory clearance of a clinical humanoid assistant requires three sequential steps that cannot be reordered, because each step generates the evidence or precedent that makes the next step possible.

**Step 1: Performance standards definition.** A joint effort among humanoid manufacturers (Unitree, Figure AI, Agility Robotics), academic medical centers (UCSD, JHU), and regulatory bodies — specifically FDA's Center for Devices and Radiological Health (CDRH) — is required to define the Special Controls that any De Novo submission for a bipedal humanoid clinical assistant must meet. The absence of these standards is the primary reason no De Novo submission has been filed: an applicant must propose and defend all Special Controls from scratch, without the support of a prior cleared device. The specific standards needed are: fall prevention performance requirements for patient-proximate environments; patient contact force limits for dynamic bipedal scenarios (extending ISO/TS 15066 to gait-induced contact events, as discussed in Section 5.2); teleoperation latency bounds for specific procedure types; and AI/ML software validation methodology for clinical manipulation policies consistent with FDA's Good Machine Learning Practice guiding principles and the December 2024 PCCP guidance.

**Step 2: IRB feasibility study generating clinical evidence.** A registered, IRB-approved feasibility study in a specific narrow indication — telemedicine-assisted physical examination is the lowest-risk candidate — generates the clinical evidence required for a De Novo submission. This study must be designed from the outset with regulatory submission in mind: pre-specified safety endpoints, a defined safety monitor protocol, and documentation of the performance standard compliance demonstrated during the study.

**Step 3: De Novo submission establishing a regulatory predicate.** The De Novo application, supported by the clinical evidence from Step 2 and the performance standards from Step 1, proposes a new product code for bipedal humanoid clinical assistants with Special Controls defined by Steps 1 and 2. First clearance establishes a predicate device, enabling subsequent 510(k) applications for derivative products and reducing the regulatory investment required for the second and third entrants.

For the EU, the parallel path requires: an ISO/TC 299 scope revision of ISO 13482 to include clinical humanoids (closing the medical device exclusion gap identified in Section 5.4), or a new ISO standard specifically for medical care robots; EU MDR Class IIa conformity assessment using the performance standards from Step 1; and EU AI Act compliance under high-risk AI obligations (effective August 2026 for new AI systems; August 2027 for AI embedded in regulated medical products). Any EU market entry must satisfy both MDR and AI Act simultaneously, a dual compliance burden for which no completed template yet exists but which is not procedurally unprecedented — regulatory precedents for navigating layered framework compliance exist in the surgical robotics domain.

### 6.5 Ethical Considerations

**Dignity of care.** Physical care interactions — patient transfer, examination, rehabilitation guidance — require that the patient feels cared for, not processed. The SPRING project finding that participants cited eye-level conversational positioning and upright posture as important for dignified interaction \cite{alamedapineda2024spring} provides direct empirical evidence that form factor affects perceived dignity of care. This finding supports the hypothesis that bipedal humanoids may have a form-factor advantage over wheeled alternatives in physical care contexts — but it remains a hypothesis requiring clinical investigation with actual patient populations. Care robotics designers must incorporate patient dignity explicitly as a design criterion alongside technical performance metrics, not as an afterthought addressed in user acceptance surveys after the engineering is complete.

**Liability and informed consent.** No current healthcare liability framework — US tort law, UK clinical negligence, EU product liability — assigns accountability for harm caused by an autonomous or semi-autonomous robotic action in a care setting. Section 5.3 documents this gap and its actuarial consequence: hospital risk departments cannot quantify institutional exposure for systems operating in legally uncharted territory. Before any human-subject deployment, consent processes for humanoid-assisted care must be defined: who explains the system to patients, what alternatives exist, and how refusal is accommodated. These are not technical problems; they require deliberate engagement between legal, ethical, and clinical stakeholders before the first human-subject study is launched, not after.

**Data privacy.** A humanoid operating in a patient room integrates cameras, microphones, depth sensors, physiological monitors, and, in future deployments, integration with EHR systems, into a single mobile platform operating in one of the most privacy-sensitive environments imaginable. HIPAA (US) and GDPR (EU) apply, but neither framework was designed for a sensor-rich mobile robot operating continuously in clinical environments. Data minimization policies, retention limits, access controls, and audit trail requirements for humanoid sensor data in clinical spaces require governance frameworks specific to this use case, developed in advance of deployment rather than retrofitted after an incident.

**Workforce framing.** The evidence does not support a displacement narrative. No clinical role exists that a humanoid currently performs reliably enough to replace a trained nurse or clinician at the current TRL. The evidence-supported framing is task offloading: routine, physically demanding, or cognitively repetitive tasks that do not require clinical judgment — patient transfer, supply retrieval, scheduled vital sign collection, movement demonstration — could be offloaded to a humanoid assistant, freeing trained clinicians for the judgment-requiring activities that constitute the core of clinical practice. The administrator survey of Imtiaz and Khan \cite{imtiaz2024perceptions} shows that the perceived risk of reduced human contact is a primary institutional barrier; communicating the task-offloading framing accurately to institutional decision-makers is as important to adoption as resolving the technical barriers.
